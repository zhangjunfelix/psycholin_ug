# Before Class: Reading a Psycholinguistic Experiment

**Preparation for:** *Introducing Experimental Design in Psycholinguistics* — classroom demonstration and hands-on experiment-building workshop  
**Research article:** Traxler (2005)  
**Submission:** Short preparation notes (approximately 250–400 words in total, plus the table in Task 3). Bring them to class.

## Why are we doing this?

In our next class, we will examine a real psycholinguistic experiment and see how a research question becomes a testable design. We will then demonstrate a **self-paced reading (SPR)** task and discuss how to construct a simplified version using an experimental platform.

You do **not** need prior experience in statistics, programming, or laboratory research. Your goal is to **understand the logic of the experiment**, not memorize all the technical details or reproduce the statistical tests.

After preparing, you should be able to:

1. Identify the main research question and why it matters for sentence processing.
2. Explain what makes a sentence **temporarily ambiguous**.
3. Distinguish **experimental conditions**, **independent variables**, and **dependent variables**.
4. Describe the major steps in a moving-window self-paced reading trial.
5. Make an evidence-based prediction about **where** reading difficulty should occur.
6. Recognize how the experiment's findings relate to theories of syntactic parsing.

## The reading

**Traxler, M. J. (2005).** Plausibility and verb subcategorization in temporarily ambiguous sentences: Evidence from self-paced reading. *Journal of Psycholinguistic Research, 34*(1), 1–30. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10936-005-3629-2

[Read the article](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10936-005-3629-2)

### How to approach the article

You do not need to read every page equally carefully. Use the following guide:

| Reading priority | Pages in the published PDF | What to focus on |
|---|---|---|
| **Essential** | **p. 1** (abstract) | Research aim and headline findings |
| **Essential** | **pp. 2–4** (introduction) | Example sentences and competing predictions |
| **Essential** | **pp. 8–11** (*Experiment*, *Method*) | Participants, materials, controls, procedure, and reading-time measurement |
| **Selective** | **pp. 12–13** (results) | Table I and the general pattern of reading times—not the ANOVA calculations |
| **Selective** | **pp. 18–21** (discussion and conclusions) | What the study concludes about initial parsing and verb preference |
| **Skim** | **pp. 21–23** (Appendix A) | What a full set of critical sentence materials looks like |

The references, complete verb-norming tables, and detailed correlational calculations are **not required** for this preparation task.

> **Reading tip:** When you find an unfamiliar technical term, first try to understand it through the example sentence. The vocabulary guide below provides a starting point.

## Key vocabulary

| Term | Working definition |
|---|---|
| **Parser** | The cognitive system or processes that construct sentence structure during comprehension |
| **Temporary syntactic ambiguity** | A word sequence that initially permits more than one structural interpretation but is resolved by later words |
| **Garden-path effect** | Processing difficulty when readers must abandon or revise an initially preferred interpretation |
| **Verb subcategorization preference** | How strongly a particular verb tends to occur with a syntactic continuation, such as a direct object |
| **Semantic plausibility** | How sensible an interpretation is given general knowledge of events |
| **Disambiguating region** | The word or region at which later input rules out an earlier syntactic analysis |
| **Self-paced reading** | A technique in which participants reveal consecutive words or regions and their reading times are recorded |

## Task 1. Understand the problem 

Read the abstract and introductory examples on pp. 1–4.

Consider this sentence from the article:

> When Susan tripped the policeman stopped and picked her up.

**Answer briefly:**

1. Before reaching *stopped*, how might a reader interpret *the policeman*?
2. What changes when the reader reaches *stopped*?
3. How might adding a comma after *tripped* help the reader?

## Task 2. Find the research question and predictions 

Use pp. 2–4 and the abstract.

1. In your own words, what is the main question Traxler investigates about **verb preference**, **plausibility**, and the timing of syntactic interpretation?
2. What might a **strong lexical-guidance account** predict when a verb strongly disfavors a direct object (for instance, *fell*)?
3. Why would difficulty at a later word provide evidence about the reader's earlier interpretation?

**Helpful comparison:**

| Sentence example | Possible mistaken interpretation |
|---|---|
| When Susan **fell** the policeman stopped ... | *Susan fell the policeman* — anomalous |
| When Susan **tripped** the table crashed ... | *Susan tripped the table* — implausible |
| When Susan **tripped** the policeman stopped ... | *Susan tripped the policeman* — plausible |

The three examples differ in **verb preference and/or plausibility**. Each can also appear with or without a comma following the subordinate verb.

<!--

{{< details "Guidance (open after attempting the task)" >}}

The study tested **60 native English-speaking college students**. Each participant read **26 critical sentences** and **60 fillers**. The three item groups differed in the subordinate verb's preferences and/or plausibility of the potential object interpretation; **different groups of 20 participants** received these item groups. Within each participant's materials, **half** of the critical sentences had a comma after the subordinate verb and **half** did not. ## Task 4. Preview the experimental procedure 

Read the *Apparatus and Procedure* subsection on p. 11.

Imagine you are participating in the experiment. Put these events in the order they occur:

- A comprehension question appears and you press Y or N.
- The computer displays the sentence with words replaced by dashes; punctuation remains visible.
- You press the spacebar and the first word becomes visible.
- Each subsequent spacebar press hides the previous word and reveals the next one.
- The program records the time spent on each displayed word.

Then answer: **Why would the researcher prefer word-by-word reading times over a single total time for the whole sentence?**

## Task 5. Make a prediction before seeing the demonstration 

Consider these two versions:

**A — Ambiguous:**

> When Sue tripped the table fell over and the vase was broken.

**B — Unambiguous:**

> When Sue tripped, the table fell over and the vase was broken.

**Make a prediction:**

1. In which version might readers have more difficulty?
2. At which word or region would you expect that difficulty to become visible: *table*, *fell*, or somewhere else?
3. How would you recognize this difficulty in the recorded data?

There is no need to guess the exact number of milliseconds. The important thing is to connect a **hypothesis about processing** to an **observable pattern**.

> **Bring your prediction to class.** We will compare it with the pattern reported in the article and with data produced by our demonstration.

## Task 6. Identify one finding (5 minutes)

Look at **Table I (p. 12)** and the beginning of the **Discussion (p. 18)**.

1. What do the reading-time columns in Table I represent?
2. What is the general effect of temporary ambiguity at the critical noun for sentences whose mistaken direct-object interpretation is implausible?
3. According to Traxler, did verb subcategorization preference **completely prevent** initial misanalysis? Or did it influence the **strength and consequences** of the analysis?

You do **not** need to explain F-values, p-values, or correlation coefficients for this assignment.

## What we will do in class

1. **Discuss the research question.** Compare how syntax-first and constraint-based explanations generate predictions.
2. **Inspect the original design.** Identify the published stimulus conditions, controls, and measured outcomes.
3. **Experience the SPR demonstration.** Read sentences by advancing one word at a time and see what data the software records.
4. **Connect data to theory.** Interpret differences in reading time at critical sentence regions.
5. **Preview the student project.** Examine a reduced classroom experiment using 16 selected critical items and two counterbalanced lists.

**Important distinction:** Our classroom experiment uses a subset of the published materials and simplifies the original design. It is useful for learning the method and testing a specific ambiguity contrast, but it is **not** a complete replication of all the plausibility and verb-preference comparisons in Traxler (2005).
